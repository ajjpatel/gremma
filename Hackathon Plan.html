01Your five features, judged one by one

Verdicts are for two different clocks: what can be demoed tomorrow in 7 hours, and what is honestly possible in a v2 product. “Impossible” below means platform-blocked, not just hard.

1 · Daily Updates — email, messages, calendar summary

Re-scope · Calendar only

Your version: read emails, messages and calendar events, speak a morning summary.

Why not as pitched

  • iOS: there is no API at all for Mail, iMessage or SMS. No permission you can request — a third-party app simply cannot read them. This is not a policy hurdle; the capability doesn't exist.
  • Android Gmail needs restricted-scope OAuth verification plus an annual CASA security assessment — 6–12 weeks and real money. Not a hackathon, not even a month.
  • Android SMS: Google Play only grants READ_SMS to apps that are the default SMS/phone/assistant handler. A vision app doesn't qualify.

What survives

  • Calendar: yes, both platforms. iOS EventKit full-access permission and Android calendar APIs are legitimate. “Your day: 3 events, first at 10, a 40-minute gap before lunch” is buildable.
  • Add reminders, weather, and the app's own data — e.g. “Your keys were last seen on the kitchen counter yesterday.”
  • v2 route for email: user-forwarded mail or a connected Gmail account via a small server — which breaks the “everything on-device” promise, so say so honestly if you pitch it.

2 · Navigation → hazard awareness — walking alerts, not turn-by-turn

Re-scope · Detector + depth, not Gemma

Your version: guide the user while walking, alert on obstacles and hazards. The re-scope you agreed to: this is hazard awareness — “something is ahead of you” — not navigation. No routes, no turn-by-turn, no steering.

Safety line you must not cross: at a normal walking pace of ~1.3 m/s, a 2-second Gemma answer means the user travels ~2.6 m past the hazard before hearing about it. Apple itself warns its own LiDAR Detection Mode “should not be relied on… for navigation.” Pitch this feature as awareness assistance, never as a replacement for a cane or guide dog — judges with accessibility experience will test exactly this.

What actually works

  • Depth first: iPhone 16 Pro Max LiDAR (effective ~5 m) via ARKit gives “something 1.5 m ahead” in milliseconds, 10×/sec, near-zero AI cost.
  • Small detector second: MediaPipe object detection runs in ~28–30 ms/frame — names the obstacle class (chair, person, bicycle).
  • Gemma last, rarely: only when the user asks “what's in front of me?” or the scene changes completely.

Platform asymmetry

  • Pixel phones have no LiDAR/ToF. ARCore depth is motion-derived and fails when standing still or facing a plain wall — the exact moments that matter.
  • So walking alerts are an iPhone-Pro-first feature. On Pixel, limit claims to detector-based awareness.
  • Outdoor turn-by-turn is already solved by Maps with VoiceOver — don't rebuild it; integrate directions and add the “last 10 metres” awareness layer Maps lacks.

3 · Record & Find — home video → “Where is item X?”

Keep · Your most original feature

Your version: record videos of the home, the model catalogues items and locations locally, later answers and guides the user to the item. Multiple videos processed in the background.

Feasible — as memory, not navigation

  • Flow that works: user records a slow pan per room and says the room name aloud → sample 1 frame/sec → detector tags common objects at ~30 ms/frame → Gemma captions only ambiguous frames → store item, room, counter/zone description, timestamp and the frame photo locally.
  • Search is cheap: EmbeddingGemma (308M, <200 MB RAM quantized) turns “blue mug” into a vector; sqlite-vec or ObjectBox retrieves it on-device. No LLM needed for lookup — Gemma only phrases the spoken answer.
  • Answer honestly in the form: “Last seen Tuesday 6:40 PM, kitchen, on the counter left of the sink.” Timestamps matter — objects move.

Where it breaks

  • “Guide me to it from where I stand” — cut it. A phone alone can't locate itself indoors: Wi-Fi positioning is 3–15 m off, BLE needs beacons, UWB needs anchors. No room identification, no indoor turn-by-turn without pre-installed infrastructure.
  • Don't caption every frame with Gemma. 1 fps × a 3-minute scan at seconds-per-frame = many minutes of processing, heat and battery. Detector-first, Gemma-for-exceptions is ~100× cheaper.
  • iOS background processing is limited and there is no background camera. Process on charge / while the app is open, and say “indexing” honestly in the UI.

4 · Visualization — “Help me picture the Taj Mahal”

Keep · Best demo on the day

Your version: detailed spoken descriptions of places, monuments and objects that build a mental picture — shape, scale, layout, materials.

Why it wins

  • This is pure language-model work — Gemma's strength, no camera latency, no safety risk, works in airplane mode, and it's the feature Seeing AI / Be My AI do least well offline.
  • Prompt pattern that makes it good: anchor to the body and familiar objects — “about 17 storeys tall; the dome is wider than two buses end to end; walk from the gate and it grows in front of you in this order…” Structure: overall shape → scale → layout → materials → one memorable detail.
  • Follow-ups are free wins: “How big is the dome compared to a house?” Multi-turn conversation is where a local model beats a one-shot description app.

Guardrails

  • Gemma's built-in knowledge is good for famous landmarks, weak for niche/local places. Prompt it to say “I don't know this place well” rather than invent architecture — a hallucinated landmark description is the fastest way to lose an accessibility judge.
  • Offer two lengths by voice: “short version” (~20 seconds spoken) and “walk me through it” (layered, pausable).

5 · Fully voice-driven experience

Keep · With platform speech, not Gemma speech

Your version: every feature reachable by voice, no need to see the screen.

Build it this way

  • Commands: platform speech-to-text (Apple Speech / Android SpeechRecognizer, forced on-device mode). Near-instant, tiny battery cost. Reserve Gemma for understanding and answers.
  • Output: AVSpeechSynthesizer / Android TTS — the same voices VoiceOver users already know and can set fast.
  • Activation: one big push-to-talk surface + VoiceOver/TalkBack gestures + headphone button. Every screen also fully operable via VoiceOver rotor — voice-only must never mean touch-broken.

Cut

  • Always-on wake word: cut. The low-power “Hey Siri / Hey Google” path (~milliwatts) is closed to third-party apps on iOS. In-app continuous listening means a permanent mic indicator, App Review scrutiny, and double-digit battery % per hour.
  • Don't run Whisper + Gemma E4B at the same time — together they pressure the 8 GB RAM ceiling. Platform STT avoids the whole problem.
  • Apple's on-device recognizer caps sessions at ~1 minute — design for short commands and re-arm listening, don't fight it.

6 · Reading — medicine labels, expiry dates, currency

Keep · Easy Gemma win, huge daily value

New in this revision — promoted from a bullet to a full feature. Small scope, daily-life value, and the safest possible Gemma workload: one still photo, one short answer, no timing pressure.

Build it this way

  • OCR reads, Gemma interprets. Apple Vision / ML Kit extracts the exact printed text first (fast, on-device). Gemma never reads pixels for text — it works from the OCR result and structures the answer: medicine name, strength, the directions as printed, expiry date.
  • Expiry is a parsing job: pull the date out of the label text and state it plainly — “Expires March 2027” — plus days remaining only if the date was read clearly.
  • Currency: read the denomination exactly as printed on the note and say it back. Never convert, never estimate value, never guess a denomination from colour or size.
  • One photo per read, spoken back in a fixed order, with a “read it again” command. That's the whole feature — which is why it will actually work on the day.

The safety link — non-negotiable

  • Medicine: the app reads the label exactly as printed and never advises whether a medicine is safe to take, what dose to take, or whether an expired item is still fine. The spoken line is: “That's what the label says — for advice on taking it, ask a pharmacist or doctor.”
  • Blurry or partial text → refuse. “I can't read that clearly — try again closer, with more light.” A misread dosage or expiry date is the worst error this app can make; the refusal is the feature working, not failing.
  • This is the safety/trust layer (§02) at its most concrete — which is why the reading beat, including a deliberate blurry-label refusal, is in the demo script.

Add / discard — from the competitive research

Add (cheap, differentiating)

  • Haptic proximity: vibration intensity rises as an obstacle nears — silent, private, works in noise. Almost no code, huge demo effect.
  • “I can't tell” as a feature: a tuned refusal prompt. Trust is the product for blind users; demo the refusal live. Full treatment in §02.
  • Reading mode: now feature 6 above — medicine labels, expiry dates and currency, offline, with refusals. Signs and menus come free from the same OCR pipeline.
  • Privacy as the pitch: home video never leaves the phone. Against Be My AI (cloud GPT-4) that's a real, checkable claim — airplane mode proves it.

Discard (already free & built-in)

  • Door / people / furniture detection — Apple Magnifier Detection Mode does it free, on-device, with LiDAR.
  • Standalone colour ID — Seeing AI and Lookout already do it well, and it demos weakly. (Currency stays: it's part of reading mode, feature 6, because the denomination is printed text the OCR can read exactly — not a colour guess.)
  • Human-volunteer calling — that's Be My Eyes' network, not a buildable feature.
  • Your own maps / outdoor routing — integrate, don't rebuild.

02The safety & trust layer — your headline differentiator

Every team at this hackathon will demo an AI that describes things. Almost none will demo an AI that refuses. For blind users, a confident wrong answer about a medicine label or an obstacle is worse than no answer — so the trust behaviour is designed, prompted, tested and scored like a feature, because it is one. Judges will ask “what happens when it's wrong?” — this section is the rehearsed answer.

The system-prompt rules (paste-ready)

These go into the narrator/reading system prompt verbatim, on both phones, and are never softened for a smoother demo:

1. NEVER estimate a distance from the camera image. Distances come only from the depth sensor. If no depth reading is provided, say you can't tell the distance.

2. NEVER read out text you cannot see clearly. If text is blurry, partial or cut off, say "I can't read that clearly" and say what would help (closer, more light, hold steady).

3. NEVER advise whether a medicine or food is safe to take or use. Read the label exactly as printed, then direct the user to a pharmacist or doctor for advice.

4. NEVER say a path is clear, a road is safe to cross, or an area is hazard-free. You may describe what you can see; absence of a detected obstacle is not proof of safety.

5. When you are unsure about anything, say "I can't tell" first, then give only what you are sure of, then say what would help you tell.

6. Describe only what is in front of the camera now. Do not invent objects, people, signs or details to complete a scene.

The spoken disclaimer — exact words, said early

Spoken once at first launch and again the first time hazard awareness is switched on in a session. Not buried in settings, not legalese:

App says, out loud

“Quick note before we start: I describe what the camera can see, and I can miss things. I'm not a replacement for your cane or guide dog. If I can't tell, I'll say so — please don't rely on me alone to stay safe.”

Demo the refusal, and score it

The refusal is a demo beat

At the 16:30 rehearsal (§08), the demo includes a deliberately blurry medicine label and one genuinely ambiguous scene. The expected, practised output is a refusal — “I can't read that clearly” — delivered as a success, not an apology. If Gemma guesses instead during rehearsal, fix the prompt before anything else.

The trust scorecard

Run 10 fixed test scenes before judging and record the split honestly: correct / incomplete / wrong / refused. Show the card on the pitch slide — e.g. “9 of 10 answered safely, 1 refusal, 0 invented.” A team that measures its own hallucinations is the rarest thing in the room, and it's what the LiteRT and accessibility judging rewards.

Say it unprompted in the pitch: “This assists awareness; it does not replace a cane, guide dog, or orientation training — and when it can't tell, it says so.” Thirty seconds of honesty up front disarms the hardest judging question instead of waiting to be caught by it.

Gemma — the only expensive piece — runs for a few seconds per user question. That workload fits both phones comfortably, offline, in airplane mode. Your compute worry is solved by architecture, not by a bigger phone.

04Your frame-delta idea — judged

Your proposal: capture the scene once, then only process what changed in the next frame, instead of reprocessing everything. Verdict: the instinct is exactly right — it's how production systems work — but the unit of “delta” can't be Gemma tokens. Here's the version that works:

EVERY ~100 MS · ~FREE

Tier 0 — Depth delta

LiDAR/ARKit depth map each frame; subtract from the last. Something new within 3 m → haptic pulse + beep pattern. No AI at all. This tier owns safety timing.

~30 MS / FRAME · CHEAP

Tier 1 — Detector delta

MediaPipe detector tracks object boxes frame-to-frame. Only a new, lost, or moved object counts as change — a chair sliding into the path triggers an alert; the same chair sitting still never re-alerts. This is your delta idea, at the object level.

SECONDS · ON DEMAND ONLY

Tier 2 — Gemma delta

Gemma runs when (a) the user asks, (b) Tier 1 sees a scene it can't classify, or (c) the overall scene embedding shifts enough to mean “new place.” It receives the changed region crop, not the full frame — smaller input, faster answer.

Why not send Gemma “frame 1 description + what changed”? Because re-describing costs the same seconds whether the input is a delta or a frame, and a vision model given a cropped delta loses the context that makes its answer safe (“…on your left” needs the whole scene). Delta at the detector and depth layers, full-frame at the language layer, rarely — you get your efficiency idea and keep the answers trustworthy. Bonus: the same scene-change signal powers Record & Find indexing (only index frames where something actually changed).

Name it correctly, every time: this three-tier system is hazard awareness — it notices new things near the user and says so. It is not navigation: no routes, no turn-by-turn, no steering decisions. And Gemma is never in the per-frame safety loop — Tiers 0 and 1 (depth + detector, milliseconds) own anything time-critical; Gemma only talks, on demand.

05Stack — and the Python question, settled

Kivy draws its own non-native widgets, which destroys the VoiceOver/TalkBack accessibility tree — disqualifying for this exact app. BeeWare is native but too immature for camera, background audio and accessibility actions. Python keeps three honourable jobs: prototyping tonight, model conversion/fine-tuning on a laptop (LiteRT Torch), and any future server logic.

Ship stack (one codebase, two platforms)

09What you're missing

Gaps in the original brief — none fatal, all cheap to close now and expensive to discover at judging. The safety items that used to live here now have their own full section (§02); they're listed as one-line reminders so nothing else in this list gets lost.

  • A blind user in the loop. Even one 20-minute call with a VoiceOver user before building beats any assumption. At minimum, test your own flows blindfolded with VoiceOver on, and never use the phrase “for the blind” in the pitch — “for blind and low-vision users.”
  • Safety stance, hallucination budget, evaluation → covered in full in §02: the rehearsed disclaimer line, the never-guess rules in the system prompt, the refusal demo beat, and the 10-scene trust scorecard. Don't duplicate them here — rehearse them there.
  • Privacy story for home video. Recording someone's home is the most sensitive data class there is. On-device-only storage, a spoken “delete everything” command, and no cloud backup — make these features, not footnotes. It's also your sharpest edge over Be My AI.
  • Battery & heat plan. Burst inference, index-on-charge, E2B default. A narrator that kills the phone by noon is worse than none — say the design choices out loud in the pitch.

The one-sentence version, if you remember nothing else
